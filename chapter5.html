Traffic systems

How SCATS and traffic lights manage city movement

Traffic lights

Traffic lights control conflict

A traffic light is a simple system that gives different road users safe, controlled turns at an intersection. The red light stops traffic, the green light allows movement, and the yellow light warns drivers that the light is about to change. This prevents vehicles from crossing at the same time in unsafe directions.

SCATS

SCATS is a smart traffic control system

SCATS stands for Sydney Coordinated Adaptive Traffic System. It is a traffic management system used to control many intersections across a city. Instead of giving every signal the same timing, SCATS monitors traffic flow and adjusts signal phases in real time based on demand.

Adaptive control

It reacts to congestion and demand

SCATS uses detectors and traffic data to see which roads are busy. If one direction has more vehicles waiting, the system can give that approach more green time. If traffic is low, it can shorten the cycle to reduce delays. This helps move traffic more smoothly during peak hours and after incidents.

Why it matters

SCATS makes traffic lights smarter, not just safer.

Traditional traffic lights follow fixed timing patterns, but SCATS is adaptive. It coordinates signals across a network so traffic can travel more efficiently, reduce queues, and respond to sudden changes like roadworks, crashes, or peak-hour demand. This means better movement for cars, buses, and pedestrians, while also improving safety and reducing unnecessary congestion.

In short, traffic lights are the basic tools of road control, and SCATS is the intelligent system that helps cities use those lights in a coordinated, responsive way.

Information

SCATS and traffic lights: key facts

SCATS stands for Sydney Coordinated Adaptive Traffic System. It is a traffic management system designed to control and coordinate signalised intersections across a city. The system was originally developed and implemented in Australia in the 1970s.

Traffic lights themselves work by controlling conflicting movements at intersections. Red stops traffic, green allows movement, and yellow warns drivers that a change is about to occur. Their job is to reduce crashes and organise the flow of vehicles and pedestrians in a safe and predictable sequence.

SCATS uses inductive loop detectors embedded in the road to detect vehicle presence and traffic flow. These detectors provide real-time information about which approaches are busy and how long vehicles are waiting. The system then adjusts the signal timings, including the cycle length, to match actual demand.

In SCATS, the cycle length is the total time for one full sequence of signal phases at an intersection. The system can shorten or lengthen this cycle depending on traffic volume, while also adjusting the green split, which is the amount of green time allocated to each movement. This allows the intersection to respond to changing conditions instead of relying only on fixed timing.

SCATS is built as a distributed computer system. It includes regional traffic control computers, local intersection controllers, and detector systems working together across a road network. This structure lets traffic signals be coordinated over a wider area, not just at a single intersection.

Another important concept in SCATS is offsets. Offsets are the timing differences between adjacent signals so that vehicles travelling at a suitable speed can move through a series of intersections without unnecessary stopping. This creates smoother traffic progression and reduces delays.

The benefits of SCATS include reduced congestion, shorter travel times, better coordination of traffic flow, improved safety, support for public transport priority, and faster responses to incidents or changing traffic patterns. It is particularly valuable in busy urban networks where traffic demand changes throughout the day.

Besides Australia, cities such as Singapore are well known for extensive adoption of SCATS. The system is used in many large urban areas because it can manage complex traffic networks more efficiently than simple fixed-time lights.
